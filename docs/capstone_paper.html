Research paper — FlyRank ML Internship, Lane 2

Refresh Opportunity Scoring: A Decision-Support Ranking for Content Review

Mehar Ali · FlyRank ML Internship, Applied Search Intelligence track

Abstract

Content teams with limited review capacity need a way to prioritize which pages to check first for refresh, but hand-written rules built on single thresholds miss most of the real opportunity. This paper builds and honestly validates a page-ranking model on FlyRank's search intelligence warehouse, comparing a transparent baseline rule against a Random Forest classifier trained on a genuine forward-looking label (features from March, outcome measured in April). Under a client-holdout validation split, the model modestly outperformed the baseline on Precision@50 (0.72 vs 0.64), while the baseline actually held a narrow edge on average precision (0.636 vs 0.617) — a result that is directional and decision-support, not a proof of causal impact. The output is delivered as a ranked action queue with reason codes, explicit human-review requirements, and a documented no-go list for what should never be automated.

Introduction / Problem statement

A content reviewer with limited time cannot manually check every page on a large site. The decision this work supports is: which pages should a reviewer look at first when review capacity is limited? The unit of analysis is one content page, scored using its trailing signals, with the output being a ranked queue a human reviewer works through — the model prioritizes, it does not act.

The cost of a wrong call is asymmetric: ranking a low-value page too highly wastes a few minutes of reviewer time; missing a genuinely declining, high-traffic page and ranking it too low means a real opportunity goes unreviewed. This asymmetry shaped the evaluation metric choice below.

Data

This work uses the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse release (build flyrank_pseudonymized_warehouse_release_v20260703), specifically the fact_content_daily_performance table, joined implicitly through client_hash_id for grouped validation.

Excluded, and why: product decision fields (health_score, priority_score, action_type) are excluded on principle — feeding a product's own decision back in as a feature would let the model simply copy that decision rather than learn real signal. Raw query, URL, title, and client-identifying fields are excluded throughout, consistent with the release's own pseudonymization. The final month of the release (_sample, June 2026) was deliberately never used for feature-building or label logic, since it is the natural outcome window for any past-to-future label and using it would leak the target into development.

Methodology

Features

Five features, each knowable strictly before the decision point: impressions_90d, clicks_90d, avg_position_90d, ctr_90d (derived from the first two), and days_since_last_seen — all aggregated only from the March feature window.

Label

A page is labeled declining if its April impressions fell more than 10% relative to its March impressions — a genuine future-outcome label, deliberately different from an earlier same-window proxy (trend_direction == "down") used in early exploratory work, which described the present rather than predicted the future.

Baseline

A transparent hand-written rule, stale_visible_ctr_gap, combining three signals confirmed against the real warehouse data before being used: staleness (pages inactive 31+ days still carry real median impressions, so staleness alone does not mean "dead page"), and the CTR-vs-position cliff (average click-through rate drops sharply from top to deep search positions, replicating an earlier finding from the starter dataset). The rule is a weighted score: 40% staleness, 30% visibility, 30% CTR gap.

Model

A Random Forest classifier (200 trees, max depth 6, balanced class weights), compared against Logistic Regression and a single Decision Tree as simpler references — so added complexity had to earn its place rather than win by default.

Validation design

Client-holdout grouped split (GroupShuffleSplit, grouped by client_hash_id): no client's pages appear in both train and test. A direct before/after comparison against a naive random split confirmed this matters — the naive split inflated every metric (AUC 0.626 vs 0.580, Precision@50 0.78 vs 0.64), because a client's other pages sitting in training let the model partially memorize client-specific patterns rather than general signal.

Leakage audit

Every feature was checked against five questions: was it calculated after the decision point (no); does the feature window overlap the label window (no — March features, April label); did any product-decision field slip in (no, none are present in this dataset); does a derived field secretly encode the label (no — ctr_90d derives only from March-only clicks and impressions); are related rows split across train/test in a way that makes the test too easy (only under the naive split, which the grouped split corrects).

Random Forest wins on Precision@50, the metric most tied to the real decision (a reviewer working through a fixed-size queue), but the baseline rule actually holds a narrow edge on average precision — meaning the baseline ranks the full list slightly better even though the model is stronger exactly at the top, where it matters most for a capacity-limited reviewer.

This is a smaller, more honest lift than an earlier same-window proxy comparison (Precision@50 0.240 → 0.740, a ~3.1x lift) — predicting a genuine future outcome is a harder problem than describing the present, and the smaller gap here reflects that the task got harder, not that the approach failed.

Error analysis

Feature importances in the Random Forest were dominated by ctr_90d (0.52), followed by clicks_90d (0.20), avg_position_90d (0.15), and impressions_90d (0.13). days_since_last_seen carried zero importance — inspection showed this feature had almost no variance in this slice, since nearly all pages were still active on the final day of the March window, leaving nothing for the model to learn from. Of 4,054 true declining pages in the test set, 2,093 (about half) were missed (false negatives); these tended toward lower measured impressions and CTR, meaning the model is weakest exactly where signal volume is thinnest — consistent with a limitation already flagged in the baseline's own top-20 review.

Limitations & honest framing

  • Validated on a single month-pair (March features → April outcome) from a client-holdout split of 35 training clients and 9 test clients — not yet tested across multiple months or seasons.
  • AUC is modest (0.53–0.59) across every method tried, meaning the ranking is a directional signal, not a confident probability estimate.
  • The staleness feature (days_since_last_seen) is currently underpowered due to low variance in this particular slice.
  • This model cannot distinguish a genuine decline from consolidation (a sibling URL absorbing traffic), seasonality, or a SERP feature change — a human reviewer must rule these out before acting on any flagged page.
  • No claim is made that a refresh action causes recovery — that would require a controlled experiment this data cannot provide. All findings here are observed and directional, not causal.

Ranked recommendations — the action playbook

The model's probability output feeds a four-tier reason-code system:

Human review is required — no-go list

  • No automatic publishing, editing, or deleting of content based on this score alone.
  • No automatic reallocation of budget or reviewer time without human sign-off.
  • No external claim that a specific page "will" decline or recover — the output is a ranked probability, not a guarantee.
  • monitor_low_priority means lower estimated risk, not zero risk.

Monitoring and retrain triggers

Re-score monthly as new data lands. Retrain if the client mix shifts meaningfully from the validated 35/9 split, if Precision@50 on a fresh held-out month drops materially below 0.64, or at minimum quarterly, since seasonal drift is untested with only one month-pair validated so far.
